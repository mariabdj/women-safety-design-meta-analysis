## Abstract

Women feel less safe than men in public space, and this changes how, when and where they use cities. Planners often respond with design: more lighting, more open sightlines, trimmed vegetation, active ground floors, good maintenance and the presence of other people. A recent meta-analysis, so far shared only as preliminary results, found that physical environment interventions reduce fear of crime on average, but gender was only tested at the study level. In my overlap search (October 2026), I found no review that compared women's and men's responses to the same design features within the same studies. This Registered Report describes a systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental, quasi-experimental and place-comparison studies that report results for women. For each of six design features, I will estimate (RQ1) the effect on women's perceived safety, (RQ2) whether this effect is larger for women than for men in the same studies, and, as an exploratory question (RQ3), whether the women-men gap in perceived safety is smaller in scenes with better overall design. A secondary question (RQ4) asks whether the same features reduce harm actually experienced by women (victimisation, harassment and crime against women) and whether this agrees with perceived safety. The smallest effect of interest is Hedges' g = 0.20. Hypotheses are tested in random-effects models that allow for dependent effects, with Holm-adjusted t-tests and equivalence tests, and every test reports what it could and could not detect. The search covers English and French records, with no date limit, in OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar, PubMed and CORE, plus theses, reports, open datasets and citation chasing.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Why women's perceived safety in public space matters

Feeling unsafe in public space is not evenly shared. In surveys, women report more fear of crime than men, for every type of offence (Ferraro, 1996). One influential explanation is the "shadow of sexual assault" hypothesis: women's fear of any crime is raised by the fear of sexual violence that may come with it (Ferraro, 1996). This fear has costs of its own. It can limit women's outdoor activities and social life, their mobility, and their quality of life and well-being (Dubey et al., 2025). Perceived safety is therefore an outcome that matters for equality in the city, not only a side effect of crime.

### 1.2 What design is thought to do

Several theories link the physical environment to feelings of safety. Prospect-refuge theory (Appleton, 1975) and its application to fear of crime (Fisher & Nasar, 1992) predict that people feel safer where they can see far (prospect), where potential offenders have few places to hide (low refuge for others) and where escape is easy (low entrapment). Natural surveillance ideas, from "eyes on the street" (Jacobs, 1961) to Crime Prevention Through Environmental Design (CPTED), predict that visibility, active frontages and the presence of other people reassure users because someone could see and help. Incivilities or "broken windows" accounts predict that litter, graffiti and disrepair signal a lack of social control and raise fear (Wilson & Kelling, 1982). Lighting is expected to act through several of these routes at night (Fotios et al., 2015).

If women's fear is partly a fear of sexual violence in places where help is out of reach, these same mechanisms might matter **more** for women than for men. Features that improve prospect, reduce hiding places and bring other people nearby would then reduce the women-men gap. But this is an assumption, not an established result.

### 1.3 What existing syntheses show and what they leave open

Several reviews summarise design and fear of crime. Lorenc et al. (2013) reviewed 47 evaluations of environmental interventions and fear of crime with a narrative synthesis, without pooling, and found little evidence that street lighting improvements reduce fear. Fotios et al. (2015) reviewed road lighting and pedestrian reassurance. Dubey et al. (2025) and te Braak and van Tienoven (2025) reviewed women's perceived safety in public places; the latter synthesised 50 studies narratively and focused on variation among women rather than on comparisons with men. Most recently, Knabe et al. (2026; preliminary results shared on OSF, not yet peer reviewed) meta-analysed 47 studies of physical environment interventions and fear of crime and report a small- to medium-sized overall effect. In that analysis, gender entered only as study-level variables (whether a study controlled for gender, and the share of women in the sample), neither of which was a significant moderator.

A study-level share of women cannot answer whether design works differently for women. A sample with 60% women and one with 40% women can show the same average effect even if women and men respond very differently inside each study. Inferring individual-level differences from group-level averages is the ecological fallacy. The question needs **within-study** comparisons: women's and men's responses to the same stimuli, measured in the same way.

### 1.4 Feeling safe and being safe

Perceived safety and actual safety do not always move together. A well-lit, open park may feel safe and still be where harassment happens, and the reverse can also be true. Randomised field studies show that some design changes reduce crime. Temporary street lights placed in New York City public housing developments reduced outdoor night-time serious crime (Chalfin et al., 2022), and restoring vacant lots in Philadelphia reduced gun violence and crime, mainly in neighbourhoods below the poverty line, while also reducing residents' safety concerns (Branas et al., 2018). A meta-analysis of 13 evaluations found that improved street lighting reduces crime (Welsh & Farrington, 2008). However, these studies report crime overall, not crime against women. A recent scoping review found no green or blue space intervention designed specifically to prevent violence against women and girls (Cleland et al., 2026). I therefore include actual safety as a secondary, mostly descriptive question (RQ4). Showing whether this evidence exists, and whether it agrees with perceived safety, is useful in itself.

### 1.5 Aims, research questions and hypotheses

The review asks which public-space design features increase women's perceived safety, and whether they reduce the women-men safety gap. It examines six features, defined in section 2.1: (1) lighting, (2) vegetation, (3) sightlines and openness, (4) active frontages and mixed use, (5) maintenance and incivilities, and (6) presence of other people.

- **RQ1.** For each feature, what is the effect on women's perceived safety?
  - **H1** (one per feature, H1a to H1f): the feature increases women's perceived safety (pooled Hedges' g > 0).
- **RQ2.** For each feature, is the effect larger for women than for men in the same studies?
  - **H2** (one per feature, H2a to H2f): the effect for women exceeds the effect for men (difference in g > 0).
- **RQ3** (exploratory, no hypothesis). Across features, is the women-men gap in perceived safety smaller in scenes with better overall design?
- **RQ4** (secondary, mostly descriptive). What is the effect of each feature on women's actual safety (victimisation, harassment, crime against women), and does it agree with the effect on perceived safety?

RQ2 and RQ3 are related but not the same. RQ2 asks, feature by feature, whether adding a feature raises women's safety more than men's. RQ3 takes each scene or place as a whole, scores how many of the features that vary within the study are in their favourable state in it (an intermediate level counts as half), and asks whether the women-men gap is smaller where the score is higher. RQ3 can use studies that compare women and men across several scenes even when no single feature was manipulated.

## 2. Methods

Reporting follows PRISMA 2020 (Page et al., 2021) and PRISMA-S (Rethlefsen et al., 2021). The protocol follows PRISMA-P (Moher et al., 2015) and NIRO-SR Part A (Topor et al., 2023), mapped item by item in Appendix A. Synthesis without pooling follows SWiM (Campbell et al., 2020). Files a reviewer needs are listed on one page in the repository README ("For reviewers").

### 2.1 Eligibility criteria

| | Inclusion | Exclusion |
|---|---|---|
| **Population** | Adults (18 or older). Samples with adolescents are included if at least 80% are adults or if adult results are reported separately | Not in the main analysis: samples that do not meet this rule (adolescent-only, child, or fewer than 80% adults without separate adult results) are kept at screening (adult_sample = no) and analysed separately (section 2.10) |
| **Exposure** | At least one of the six design features, defined below, varied between conditions or places | Features that are not physical design (policing, CCTV, apps, campaigns, self-defence training) |
| **Comparator** | The same scene, route or place in the less favourable state of the feature (defined below); or a matched place that differs in the feature | No comparison condition |
| **Outcome (primary)** | Self-reported perceived safety, fear of crime or reassurance, on any rating scale or as a proportion feeling safe | Behaviour or behavioural intention only (avoidance, route choice), physiological measures only |
| **Outcome (secondary, RQ4)** | Harm experienced by women: victimisation, sexual harassment, sexual assault, crime against women; from surveys, police records or systematic observation | Crime counts not broken down by victim sex, unless women's counts can be obtained (section 2.5) |
| **Study design** | Experiments (photos, video, virtual reality, field manipulations), quasi-experiments (before-after with or without control, matched places), and place-comparison studies in which defined places differ in a feature | Purely correlational ratings of one's "neighbourhood" as a whole; qualitative-only studies; reviews (used for citation chasing only) |
| **Must report** | Results for women separately (perceived safety: ideally women and men), or data allowing them to be computed (section 2.5) | Pooled results only, after the author contact procedure fails |
| **Language and dates** | English or French; any publication date up to the date of the search | Other languages (counted and listed as "excluded: language") |

A study needs at least one eligible outcome: perceived safety, actual safety, or both.

**Feature definitions and the favourable state.** Each feature is coded so that the "treatment" is the state that theory predicts to be safer, and g > 0 means safer in that state:

1. **Lighting:** more light (illuminance) or more uniform light, compared with less or less uniform light. Light colour and lamp type are recorded (column `light_type`) and described narratively, not used in H1a.
2. **Vegetation:** low, trimmed or open planting that keeps views clear, compared with dense or eye-level planting that blocks views. Contrasts of "more greenery" against "less greenery" with no change in views are reported narratively, with no directional hypothesis.
3. **Sightlines and openness:** longer and wider views, fewer hiding places (concealment), more escape routes (less entrapment).
4. **Active frontages and mixed use:** more shop fronts, windows, entrances or ground-floor activity facing the space, or more mixed use.
5. **Maintenance and incivilities:** less litter, graffiti, vandalism, disrepair or abandoned buildings.
6. **Presence of other people:** more people present, or the presence of women, families or mixed groups, compared with an empty space. Contrasts that add only a group of men are reported narratively.

A contrast that cannot be mapped onto one of these favourable states (for example tree species) is described narratively and not pooled. A manipulation that changes several features at once is described narratively, outside the per-feature models. Both are recorded in the narrative table (Appendix C4), not in the pooled sheet.

**Gender.** I use the participants' reported gender or sex as given in each study and record which was measured. Most studies report two categories; results for other genders are extracted where reported, in the narrative table (Appendix C4), and described narratively. This is a limitation of the evidence, not a choice of this review.

### 2.2 Information sources and search strategy

**Sources.** OpenAlex (title and abstract search through the API), Semantic Scholar (bulk search), PubMed, and CORE (open repositories, including theses and reports; title search through its website). I do not have institutional access to Scopus or Web of Science; OpenAlex covers a broad multidisciplinary set of records, and citation chasing catches what keyword searches miss. Other sources (Google Scholar, thesis repositories, preprint servers, UN Women and CIE reports) and their exact queries are in Appendix B. No date or language limit is applied at the interface.

**Strategy.** The final set is the union, after deduplication, of four combinations of word blocks (Appendix B): S1 = design features AND perceived safety; S2 = design features AND harm terms AND women/gender; S3 = French outcome terms; S4 = perceived safety AND women/gender AND place terms (for gender-focused studies whose abstract names no specific feature).

**Development and test.** Before testing, I fixed a set of seven benchmark primary studies likely to be eligible, chosen from titles only (Adamczak et al., 2025; Baran et al., 2018; Başaran et al., 2026; Basu et al., 2021; Boomsma & Steg, 2014; Brands et al., 2025; Navarrete-Hernández et al., 2025); their eligibility will be judged at screening like any other record. Version 1 used generic words and returned mostly irrelevant records. Version 3 (31 October 2026, after adding common phrasings such as "feel unsafe" and "safety perceptions") returned 13,641 records in OpenAlex, 8,624 in Semantic Scholar and 1,126 in PubMed (union of S1, S2 and S4; S3 is counted separately, Appendix B2), and retrieved 7 of 7 benchmarks in OpenAlex and all 6 indexed in Semantic Scholar. None is indexed in PubMed; there a known relevant record (Lorenc et al., 2013) was retrieved with the version 2 strings. I re-ran the version 2 strings by hand in the public OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar and PubMed pages, and all 14 counts I could check matched the scripted runs; I will re-run the final strings by hand in the same way before the full search. There was no peer review of the search by another person; I applied the PRESS 2015 checklist (McGowan et al., 2016) myself (Appendix B). In CORE only searches on the title field work reliably and the site misreads queries that join whole strategies, so each strategy is run separately there (Appendix B3); 2 of the 7 benchmarks are indexed in CORE. As a second, independent recall check at Stage 2, I report the share of eligible primary studies cited by Knabe et al. (2026) and te Braak and van Tienoven (2025) that the search retrieved. No known primary study of harm to women was available as a benchmark, which is consistent with Cleland et al. (2026).

**Citation chasing.** Backward and forward citations of every included study and of the main earlier reviews (Cleland et al., 2026; Dubey et al., 2025; Fotios et al., 2015; Knabe et al., 2026; Lorenc et al., 2013; te Braak & van Tienoven, 2025), through Lens.org with the `citationchaser` R package.

**Logging and update.** Every search is logged with date, source, interface, exact string, filters and number of records at the time it is run, and raw exports are kept unchanged. The full search runs only after in-principle acceptance. If more than six months pass before the analysis starts, the database searches are re-run.

### 2.3 Study selection

**Records management.** Records are imported into Zotero and deduplicated by DOI, then by normalised title and year (`synthesisr` R package), with a manual check for near-duplicates.

**My screening.** I screen titles and abstracts in ASReview (van de Schoot et al., 2021), which ranks records by likely relevance. I stop when at least 30% of records have been screened, the last 100 consecutive records were irrelevant, and every benchmark study retrieved by the search has been screened. These values are stricter than the example values of the SAFE procedure, which are at least 10% of records screened and no relevant record in the last 50 (Boetje & van de Schoot, 2024).

**Second screening by an AI model (single author).** I am the only author of this review. A large language model screens 100% of titles and abstracts and 100% of full texts, blind to my decisions, with the frozen prompt in Appendix C5. Each record is sent in a separate session that contains only the prompt and that record, with no conversation history and no access to my decision files; the model identifier, the date of each call, the prompt version and every output are archived. Before it is used, I validate it on the seven benchmarks plus the first 300 records I screen in ASReview (which are enriched for relevant records), adding further records until the set contains at least 20 records I include: the model must include all seven benchmarks and at least 95% of the records I include. If it fails, I revise the prompt once, record the change as a deviation and validate again; if it fails twice, it is not used and I screen 100% of records myself. Every record that either the model or I include goes to full-text screening, including records the model includes after my stopping point, so my stopping rule never limits the set of records that is checked. At full text, the model's decisions on the first 30 full texts are compared with mine and the agreement is reported before its other full-text decisions are used; I decide every full text myself. I also re-screen a random 20% of the titles and abstracts I screened and a random 20% of full texts, at least seven days later, without seeing my first decisions. For both comparisons I report Cohen's κ (McHugh, 2012), percent agreement and recall of my inclusions; with few relevant records κ can be low when agreement is high, so all three are reported. Agreement with the model is computed on the records screened by both the model and me. If κ is below 0.60 in either comparison, I check every disagreement against the full text; if any record I had excluded is then judged eligible, I revise the manual and re-screen every record I had screened at that stage. I make every final inclusion decision against the full text, and keep a decision log.

**Full-text exclusions.** Each excluded full text receives one reason from a fixed list, applied in this order: not English or French; not a primary study; no eligible design; no eligible feature; no eligible outcome; no results for women (after author contact); duplicate sample; no usable data.

### 2.4 Data extraction

**Unit.** One row per effect size, with study, sample, condition and outcome identifiers (variables in Appendix C4). `pair_id` links exactly one women's effect and one men's effect that share the same scene, item and contrast; it is unique within a study, feature and gender, and left empty when there is no match. The extraction sheet is piloted on three studies and then frozen; later changes are reported as deviations. For the three pilot studies, I also compute the effect sizes by hand and compare them with the scripts.

**Extracted information:** study (authors, year, country, setting, design type, stimulus type, time of day); sample (N per group and condition, by gender; age); features and their favourable state (2.1); outcomes (measure, scale range, direction); results (means, SDs or SEs with the type recorded, test statistics, proportions, counts) with the page, table or figure of each number; whether the study is flagged as seen before Stage 1 (2.13).

**Double extraction (single author).** I extract all data. The same model independently re-extracts 100% of the numbers from the full texts with the frozen prompt in Appendix C6. It does not see my numbers, risk-of-bias ratings or other coded values: it receives only the row identifiers of my sheets (`es_id`, `study_id`, `sample_id`, `contrast`, `feature`, `gender`, `pair_id` and `outcome_measure`; `gap_id`, `study_id`, `scene_id` and `scene_name` for the gap sheet; `act_id`, `study_id`, `contrast`, `feature` and `outcome_measure` for the harm sheet), where `contrast` names the two conditions compared as the paper names them, so that the two sheets can be matched, and it adds a row for any comparison it finds that is not on the list. Before I open the model's output, I freeze a copy of my sheets and record its checksum. A script (`verification/verify_double_extraction.py`) compares the two. Before resolution I report, for each study, the share of numbers filled by at least one of us that agree exactly; if it is below 90% for a study, I re-extract that study in full, repeat the comparison, report the new agreement whatever it is and resolve the remaining differences as below. Rows that only the model extracted, or that it missed, count as disagreements until resolved; a row I add after the frozen copy is made must also be extracted by the model. Numbers printed in a source other than the full text (author replies, theses or reports; 2.5) are re-extracted by the model from that same source, which is attached with the full text. Numbers I compute from an open dataset are computed by a script and recomputed by a second, independent script; the model checks only numbers that are printed in a source. Numbers read from a figure are not re-extracted by the model and are left out of the exact-agreement share; they are checked by my two digitisations (see Figures). I resolve every difference against the source and record each decision, with its page, in a resolution log; the script checks that every change to my sheet after freezing is in that log. Entries in the narrative table (Appendix C4), and any note in which the model says that a listed feature or favourable state does not match its contrast, are compared by reading, and differences are resolved and logged in the same way.

**Figures.** When numbers appear only in a figure, I read them with WebPlotDigitizer (Drevon et al., 2017), twice, and check that the digitised values reproduce any number printed in the text. I use the mean of the two readings; if they differ by more than 2% of the axis range, I read the figure a third time and use the median of the three. All readings are archived with the extraction files, and source_location names the figure and the columns read from it.

**RQ3 scene scores (exploratory).** Within each study, a feature is coded 1 in a scene that shows its more favourable state (2.1) and 0 in a scene that shows its less favourable state; a scene at an intermediate level of a feature with more than two levels is coded 0.5, and a feature that does not vary within the study is coded 0 in all its scenes. The scene score is the sum of the six codes (0 to 6). Scores are therefore compared only within a study. Coding uses a file that contains only the stimulus images and method descriptions, with results removed. The model and I each code from that file (the model with the frozen prompt in Appendix C7, and the list of `scene_id` and `scene_name` values of the gap sheet, so that the two codings can be matched); percent agreement and unweighted Cohen's κ per feature are reported, over the scenes of the studies in which that feature varies according to at least one coder (if neither coder codes the feature as varying in any study, no agreement is reported for it; if κ cannot be computed, only percent agreement is reported), and I resolve disagreements against that file before I extract that study's results; I may have seen those results at full-text screening (2.3).

### 2.5 Missing data and evidence beyond journal articles

Many studies measured women and men but reported only the total. To recover these results, I use, in this order: (1) the study's own open data (OSF, Zenodo, Figshare, Dryad, supplements), from which I compute women's and men's results; (2) theses, conference papers and reports of the same study; (3) author contact, with one email and one reminder after 14 days, closing 28 days after the first email, each contact logged.

A non-article source is used only if it is traceable (DOI or stable URL, access date), published by a journal, an official statistics body, a recognised data archive or the study's own authors, documented, licensed for reuse (or permitted in writing), and passes a sanity check: from the data I reproduce at least one number the source itself published. Otherwise it is not used and the reason is logged. Such sources are listed in a source register (Appendix E). Large official surveys, such as the European Social Survey, are used only for context and never pooled. If the data for an effect size cannot be obtained, the study is described narratively and counted in the PRISMA flow.

### 2.6 Risk of bias

I adapt the revised JBI critical appraisal tool for quasi-experimental studies (Barker et al., 2024) to six items, rated yes, no or unclear with a supporting quote or page: (1) the sample and its recruitment are described; (2) conditions are comparable except for the feature (same scene, time and viewpoint, or matched places); (3) the order of conditions is randomised or counterbalanced, or allocation is random; (4) the safety measure is an established scale or a clearly worded item about safety or fear; (5) outcome data are complete, or missing data are explained; (6) the analysis is appropriate and the reported statistics are consistent. **Overall:** high risk if item 2 or item 4 is "no"; low if all six are "yes"; some concerns otherwise. The model rates all studies independently (Appendix C6); I freeze my ratings before I open the model's and record their checksum, and agreement per item (percent agreement and unweighted Cohen's κ; when both raters use one single rating for every study, for example all "yes", κ is not defined and only percent agreement is reported) is reported before I resolve differences. I use JBI rather than RoB 2 or ROBINS-I because most eligible studies are rating experiments with images, which those tools do not fit; the adaptation is not validated, and I report this as a limitation. For RQ4, item 2 becomes "comparable control area or pre-period" and item 4 "harm recorded in the same way before and after, or in the places compared"; a study that reports both outcomes is rated once for each (perceived safety and harm), and a study kept only in the narrative table is rated too, in the domain of the outcome it reports (perceived safety, harm, or both).

### 2.7 Effect sizes

All effect sizes are computed by script and checked by a second implementation of the formulas (agreement within 10⁻⁸). Where a study reports its own d or g, my value is compared with it; any difference above 0.05 is checked against the source and explained in the notes.

**Direction.** g > 0 always means "safer in the favourable state" (2.1). Scales that measure fear are reversed. Reported standard errors are converted to standard deviations (SD = SE × √n) before any formula.

**Formulas (perceived safety):**

- Between-subject designs: standardised mean difference with the exact small-sample correction (Hedges, 1981), `escalc(measure = "SMD")` in metafor.
- Within-subject designs: standardised mean change with raw-score standardisation by the control SD (Becker, 1988), `escalc(measure = "SMCR")`. When the correlation between conditions is not reported, I use r = 0.5, with 0.3 and 0.7 as sensitivity analyses.
- Between-subject t statistics are converted with d = t × √(1/n₁ + 1/n₂), then corrected to g. Odds ratios with a 95% CI are converted with d = ln(OR) × √3/π (Chinn, 2000), variance var(ln OR) × 3/π², and the resulting d is used without a small-sample correction. Proportions feeling safe are first turned into an odds ratio from the 2 × 2 table of counts (0.5 added to every cell if any cell is 0) and then converted in the same way; this conversion will be coded and tested before extraction (Appendix F).
- Studies that report only an F with one numerator degree of freedom (t = √F) or a within-subject t are converted with the formulas of Lakens (2013): a within-subject t gives d_rm = t × √(2(1 − r)/n) (Lakens, 2013, equation 9 with d_z = t/√n), using the reported r or, if it is not reported, the same imputed r (0.5; 0.3 and 0.7 in sensitivity analyses); this assumes equal SDs in the two conditions, so the result is on the SMCR scale, and its variance uses the SMCR formula; a study that reports only an exact p-value is first turned into t with the study's degrees of freedom. For t from F or from p, the sign is taken from the direction reported in the study (means, text or figure); p-values are taken as two-sided unless the study states otherwise. These conversions are added to both implementations and tested before extraction starts (Appendix F); every conversion is recorded in `es_method`.

**Choice of effects within a study.** When a feature has more than two levels, I compare the most favourable with the least favourable level. In factorial designs I use the main effect of the feature averaged over the other factors; if only simple effects are reported, I use all of them as effects of the same study. When a study reports a composite scale and its items, I use the composite; otherwise every eligible item or measure is an effect of the same study. With several follow-up times, I use the first measurement after the change. Effects that share participants or a control condition are kept, and their dependence is handled by the model (2.8).

**Place-comparison studies** enter the pooled models only if they report means and SDs (or proportions) for each place and gender. Regression coefficients adjusted for covariates are not converted into g; such studies are described narratively. Design type is recorded for every effect and tested in a sensitivity analysis (2.10).

**Gap (OC1 and RQ3):** the standardised mean difference between men's and women's ratings of the same scene (positive = women feel less safe than men).

**Actual safety (RQ4):** for counts before and after with a control area, the relative effect size of Welsh and Farrington (2008), RES = (a·d)/(b·c), where a and b are the counts in the treated area before and after, and c and d those in the control area. I analyse ln(RES) with variance 1/a + 1/b + 1/c + 1/d under a Poisson assumption (if a count is 0, 0.5 is added to all four counts of that comparison); a value above 0 means relatively less harm in the treated area. Because area crime counts are often more variable than a Poisson process (Welsh & Farrington, 2008), a sensitivity analysis doubles every variance. Survey prevalence data, and harm counts without a control area (before-after only, or places compared at one time), are described in the narrative table (SWiM) and not pooled. The adult rule of 2.1 is not applied to RQ4 area counts: counts of harm against women are used as reported, and the victims' age range, if given, is recorded and described. RQ4 effects are never pooled with perceived-safety g values.

### 2.8 Synthesis

**Model.** Effects from the same study often share participants, so their sampling errors are correlated. For each pooled estimate of perceived safety (RQ1, RQ2, OC1 and RQ3) I use a correlated-and-hierarchical-effects model (Pustejovsky & Tipton, 2022): random effects for studies and for effects within studies, estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML), and an assumed correlation of 0.6 between the sampling errors of effects from the same study (`vcalc()`, then `rma.mv(yi, V, random = ~ 1 | study_id / es_id, test = "t", dfs = "contain")` in metafor; Viechtbauer, 2010). Tests use t-distributions with degrees of freedom from the containment method, which for a pooled effect equal the number of studies minus 1. With only 3 to 5 studies, the two variance components cannot be estimated precisely; I report them but do not interpret them. If the model does not converge, it is refitted with a second optimiser (BFGS) and, if that fails, with study-level random effects only; the model used is reported for every estimate. When at least 10 studies contribute, I repeat inference with CR2 cluster-robust standard errors (Pustejovsky & Tipton, 2018). The model-based test decides; CR2 is a robustness check (2.10).

**When to pool.** A pooled estimate needs at least 3 studies. Otherwise the results are summarised following SWiM (direction of each study's effect); no formal test is done. Features with fewer than 3 studies are reported as "not tested".

**Analyses for each question:**

- **RQ1 (H1a to H1f):** one model per feature, women's effects only.
- **RQ2 (H2a to H2f):** for every women-men pair of effects (same scene, item and contrast; `pair_id`, section 2.4), I compute g(women) − g(men), with variance v(women) + v(men) because the two groups are different people, and I pool these differences per feature with the same model. Every comparison is therefore within a study, and variation in the gender difference between studies is estimated rather than assumed to be zero.
- **RQ3 (exploratory):** gap effects regressed on the scene score, split into each study's mean score and each scene's deviation from it; I report the within-study slope with its 95% CI from CR2 cluster-robust standard errors, only if at least 10 studies give gaps for at least two scenes with different scores.
- **RQ4:** for counts, a random-effects model (REML) of ln(RES) per feature with the Knapp-Hartung adjustment, one effect per study (if a study gives several, the one with the most events), when at least 3 comparable studies exist, otherwise SWiM. I also compare, feature by feature, the direction of the perceived-safety effect with the direction of the actual-safety effect where both are pooled; where one of them is not pooled, its SWiM direction count is reported next to the other outcome's result.

**Reported for every model:** number of studies, number of effects, N (the sum over studies of the largest sample contributing to one effect in each study; a lower bound for multi-arm studies), pooled estimate, 95% CI, 95% prediction interval (IntHout et al., 2016; computed with the model's degrees of freedom, k − 1, as in IntHout et al.), τ² and I² at each level (Higgins & Thompson, 2002), df and p.

**Multiple testing.** H1 and H2 are two families. Within each family, Holm's method is applied over the features that reach at least 3 studies; the family size is the number of features tested.

### 2.9 Decision rules and sensitivity of the tests (sampling plan)

**Smallest effect size of interest (SESOI).** g = 0.20 for RQ1 and a difference of 0.20 for RQ2, for two reasons. First, resources: with the number of studies I can expect (below), smaller effects could neither be detected nor ruled out, so a smaller SESOI would make every test uninformative (Lakens, 2022). Second, size in practice: on a 7-point safety item with, for example, an SD of 1.5, g = 0.20 is a change of about 0.3 points, which I take as the smallest change a design guideline should rely on. This second reason is a judgement; I therefore report every confidence interval in full.

**Decision rule for each H1 and H2**, applied in this order with Holm-adjusted p-values (two-sided tests, so that an effect in the opposite direction can also be detected):

1. Significant and also equivalent (both tests below .05): "present but smaller than the SESOI", with its direction stated; not counted as support.
2. Significant and positive: "supported". Significant and negative: "opposite direction".
3. Equivalent (two one-sided tests against ±0.20; Lakens et al., 2018): "no meaningful effect".
4. Anything else: "inconclusive", reported with the minimal detectable effect for the observed number of studies at both Holm bounds.

**Sensitivity analysis.** I cannot choose how many studies exist, so the sampling plan states what a given number of studies can detect, for a random-effects t-test with one effect per study (Hedges & Pigott, 2001; Valentine et al., 2010), at α = .05 (the least strict Holm threshold) and α = .05/6 = .0083 (the strictest Holm threshold with six features). Typical studies: within-subject, 60 women rating both conditions with r = 0.5 (sampling variance about 0.017); between-subject, 40 women per condition (about 0.05). Results with I² = 50% (full grid, I² from 0 to 75%, and code in `00_protocol/sensitivity/`, checked by a second implementation):

| Scenario (I² = 50%) | Studies for 80% power at g = 0.20, α = .05 | Same, α = .0083 | Power of the equivalence test with 10 studies and a true effect of 0, α = .05 / .0083 |
|---|---|---|---|
| RQ1, within-subject | 9 | 14 | 0.86 / 0.39 |
| RQ1, between-subject | 22 | 34 | 0.13 / 0.00 |
| RQ2, within-subject | 16 | 24 | 0.45 / 0.00 |
| RQ2, between-subject | 42 | 65 | 0.00 / 0.00 |

**Expected informativeness.** Lighting is likely the most studied feature: it is the most frequent category of intervention across the effect sizes of the most recent meta-analysis (Knabe et al., 2026; from its table of interventions, in its bottom-up grouping, not from its results). I cannot yet say how many studies report results for women: Knabe et al. (2026) include 47 studies of all features, and how many of them report women separately is unknown until screening. I expect most experiments in this field to use within-subject ratings. I therefore expect an informative test of H1 for lighting, and an inconclusive result to be the most likely outcome for RQ2 and for the less-studied features. Such a result is still informative: it is reported with what could and could not be ruled out, and it documents how much evidence is missing for women.

### 2.10 Heterogeneity, small-study effects and sensitivity analyses

**Exploratory moderators** (only for features with at least 10 studies, labelled exploratory): stimulus type (photo, video, VR, field), time of day (day, night), design type (experiment, quasi-experiment, place comparison).

**Small-study effects** (only for features with at least 10 studies): a funnel plot and an Egger-type test inside the model, with the modified precision √((n₁ + n₂)/(n₁n₂)) for between-subject effects and √(2(1 − r)/n) for within-subject effects as moderator (Egger et al., 1997; Pustejovsky & Rodgers, 2019; Rodgers & Pustejovsky, 2021). A significant result is read as "small-study effects", which can have causes other than publication bias.

**Sensitivity analyses** (H1 and H2, every feature with a pooled estimate): sampling-error correlation 0 (the three-level model; Assink & Wibbelink, 2016), 0.3 and 0.8; r = 0.3 and 0.7 for within-subject effects; without high risk-of-bias studies; without data from sources other than the article itself (authors, open datasets, theses or reports; section 2.5); experiments and quasi-experiments only; without the studies flagged as seen before Stage 1 (2.13); without effects whose control-condition mean is less than 10% of the scale range from either end of the scale (2.11); CR2 standard errors; and leaving out each study in turn. A decision is labelled **robust** if the full rule of 2.9, with the same Holm adjustment, gives the same decision in every one of these analyses. If a feature cannot be tested in one of them (fewer than 3 studies remain), its decision is labelled "robustness not assessable"; leaving out each study is done only when at least 4 studies contribute. In every sensitivity analysis, the Holm family size is that of the main analysis. The CR2 analysis is part of this set only for features with at least 10 studies (both tests then use the CR2 standard errors); for features with fewer studies, the label is based on the other analyses, and this is stated next to the decision. Results with the samples coded adult_sample = no added are a separate report with the same family size, unless adding them makes a further feature testable, in which case that feature is added to the family. For RQ2, for effects computed from means and SDs, I also report the women-minus-men difference in raw mean change expressed as percent of the maximum possible score (POMP; Cohen et al., 1999), so that the reader can see whether differences in SD between women and men, or women's lower starting point, drive the result. This check has no equivalence bound, so it is reported beside the decision and not used in the robust label.

### 2.11 Outcome-neutral checks

These checks do not depend on the answers to RQ1 to RQ4.

1. **OC1, positive control (gender gap).** In the control conditions of the included studies (samples eligible for the main analysis, 2.1), women should report lower perceived safety than men, a well-documented finding (Ferraro, 1996). I pool the baseline gap (positive = women feel less safe; 2.7) with the model of 2.8. If its 95% CI is entirely above 0, RQ2 and RQ3 are interpreted as planned. If not, RQ3 is reported as uninformative, RQ2 decisions carry the label "positive control not met", and no conclusion that a feature reduces the gender gap appears in the abstract or the conclusions. If fewer than 3 studies give baseline data for both genders, the check is reported as not possible, and RQ2 and RQ3 are reported with that caution stated.
2. **Ceiling and floor.** Effects whose control-condition mean lies less than 10% of the scale range from either end of the scale are flagged and removed in a sensitivity analysis; rows without a control mean cannot be flagged and are kept.
3. **Search sensitivity.** The final search must retrieve all seven benchmarks and every eligible seed study listed in 2.13; any miss leads to a revised string, a re-run and a logged deviation.
4. **Data quality.** Screening agreement as in 2.3; exact agreement between the two extractions before resolution, reported per study, with full re-extraction of any study below 90% (2.4); effect sizes reproduced by two implementations.

Analysis code tested on simulated data:** `05_analysis/analysis.R` implements every confirmatory analysis, OC1, the exploratory RQ3 slope, RQ4 and every sensitivity analysis of section 2.10, for the effect-size methods already coded (means, between-subject t, odds ratios, reported SEs). `05_analysis/simulate_data.R` creates datasets with known true effects (means with SDs or SEs, and between-subject t statistics) and `05_analysis/test_pipeline.R` checks that the code recovers them; the odds-ratio conversion is compared between the two independent implementations (`verification/verify_effect_sizes.R`) on every odds-ratio row, and was tested on a small set of odds-ratio rows (`05_analysis/simulated/or_ci_check/`). Every RQ1 and RQ2 estimate fitted with the CHE model is re-estimated independently, from the matrix formulas with separate REML code, in `verification/verify_model.R`. Over 300 simulated reviews, each simulating 8 studies per feature, I fitted the lighting models after setting aside samples coded adult_sample = no; RQ2 then used 3 to 8 studies with women-men pairs (median 6), and the number of RQ1 studies was not recorded (`verification/verify_coverage.R`; runs in `05_analysis/simulated/coverage_runs.csv`). Over these runs, the ratio of the mean model standard error to the spread of the estimates was 0.91 for RQ1 and 0.98 for RQ2. The 95% intervals covered the true value in 93.7% of runs for RQ1 (exact 95% CI 90.3% to 96.1%, consistent with 95%) and in 97.7% for RQ2 (95.3% to 99.1%). For RQ1 the model standard errors were about 10% smaller than the spread of the estimates, but the t-based intervals still covered the true value at a rate consistent with 95%. For RQ2, where heterogeneity is small, the t distribution with k − 1 df makes the intervals slightly wider than needed; this costs some power and does not raise the false-positive rate. Before extraction starts, and before any real data are opened, I add and test the remaining conversions (F, p, within-subject t, proportions), the exploratory moderator models and the figures (forest and funnel plots).
